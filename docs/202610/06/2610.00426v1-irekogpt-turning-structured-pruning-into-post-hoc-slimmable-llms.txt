# IREKO GPT: Turning Structured Pruning into Post-Hoc Slimmable LLMs 

Pietro Moriello Pietro Buzzega Angelo Porrello Simone Calderara 

AImageLab, University of Modena and Reggio Emilia, Italy 

{name.surname}@unimore.it 

Abstract 

We introduce I REKO GPT, a post-hoc method for converting pretrained LLMs into slimmable models whose width can be adjusted at inference time. Building on SliceGPT, we retain its projection matrices without pruning them, allowing a single model to expose nested subnetworks at different widths. We improve robustness by calibrating each layer across multiple compression ratios, and correct downstream linear layers through gradient-free ridge regression. Across Llama and Qwen models, preliminary results show improvements over naive PCA-based slimming, with the largest gains at high compression. Code is available at https://github.com/aimagelab/IrekoGPT 

1 Introduction 

Deploying large neural networks on resource-constrained devices, such as mobile phones or embedded systems, requires effective compression techniques – such as weight quantization Frantar et al. [2023] and network pruning Han et al. [2016] – to enable efficient inference and democratize access to AI. Among these, structured pruning Ma et al. [2023], Ashkboos et al. [2024] enables substantial savings without relying on dedicated hardware support, as compressed models can be obtained through simple operations in weight space, such as slicing the original weight matrices. Some structured approaches merge redundant neurons with similar structure Ma et al. [2023], Verma et al. [2026], while others leverage eigenvector decompositions to identify the least important components to remove Wang et al. [2025], Ashkboos et al. [2024]. The resulting networks retain most of their original functionality while being lighter to store and faster. However, these approaches typically optimize the pruned architecture for a predefined target size (e.g., 80% of the original model). In real-world scenarios, resource availability is rarely static or known a priori: it may fluctuate, for instance, depending on the device’s current power-saving mode or on the workload it is required to handle at a given time. In these cases, structured pruning would require maintaining a separate compressed network for each resource budget, incurring additional storage costs and runtime overhead from loading and unloading different models. To overcome these limitations, pioneering works on slimmable neural networks Yu et al. [2019], Yu and Huang [2019] train a single model to operate at multiple widths. However, this capability is typically learned from scratch, which conflicts with the growing reliance on pretrained foundation models. In this paper, we address the problem of converting pretrained large language models into slimmable variants, as recently explored in Wong et al. [2026], Zaccone et al. [2026], Rauba and van der Schaar [2026]. In particular, we revisit the established structured pruning method SliceGPT Ashkboos et al. [2024] and show that its formulation can be naturally extended to support slimmable inference. Building on this connection, we introduce a set of modifications that better align the activations of smaller subnetworks with those of the original full-width model. We show that the resulting approach yields particularly pronounced gains at higher compression levels.

3 Method 

3.1 Revisiting SliceGPT: from slicing to slimming 

Our first observation is that SliceGPT can be turned into a slimmable model by absorbing the full eigenvector matrix Qℓ into the model weights (hence, without slicing). Indeed, since the columns of each Qℓ are ordered by decreasing eigenvalue, this induces a nested structure : retaining the first d

columns always preserves the most important principal directions. Different widths can therefore be obtained simply by truncating each projection to a different number of columns, while ensuring that smaller subnetworks are nested within larger ones and retain the highest-variance components. This establishes a direct connection between SliceGPT and slimmable networks: the ordered PCA basis used for structured pruning can instead be retained in full and exploited to support dynamic width selection at inference time. This observation forms the core foundation of our approach. 

3.2 Improving slimmability by expanding the calibration dataset 

To improve slimmability, we revisit the sequential nature of SliceGPT described above. In the original approach, once the transformation at layer ℓ has been computed, its output is propagated to the next layer and therefore determines the activations used to construct the calibration set for layer ℓ + 1 .These activations define the PCA basis and ultimately the compression subspace of the next layer. In a slimmable model, however, the output of layer ℓ is not fixed at inference time: it can be dynamically reduced according to different width ratios. This raises a fundamental question: which width should be propagated when calibrating the next layer? If calibration is performed only at full width, while inference later operates at, e.g., 60% width, the downstream layers are optimized on activations drawn from a different distribution than the one they will actually receive at inference time. We therefore expect a shift in the activations used to compute PCA at subsequent layers. We introduce a simple modification to ensure that PCA captures the distributional shifts induced when the network operates at multiple width configurations. The key idea is to expand the calibration set with activations collected at different widths. In particular, we define a set of compression ratios 

R = {r1, . . . , r K } ⊂ (0 , 1) . Given the original calibration dataset X , we maintain K + 1 parallel activation streams, one for each compression ratio in ¯R = R ∪ { 1}, where 1 denotes the full-width model. After computing the PCA basis at layer ℓ − 1, we truncate it according to each ratio rk ∈ ¯R

3.3 Leveraging full-width signals for slim subnetworks 

As discussed in Sec. 2, compression only affects a subset of components in the model. Several linear layers – e.g. , the value projection in self-attention – retain their full output dimension even when their input is compressed; that is, their output width dout remains equal to the original hidden size. We exploit these width-preserving points to introduce a representation-level constraint. Specifically, we adjust the corresponding weight matrices so that, when fed with activations from compressed streams, their outputs remain close in MSE to those produced by the full-width model. In this way, full-width activations serve as reference signals for correcting representations of slim subnetworks. In formal terms, let Ik ∈ RB×dk denote the input activations produced by the k-th compressed stream, where dk is the corresponding compressed width, and let O ∈ RB×dout denote the full-width target outputs, with dout equal to the original hidden size. We seek a residual correction ∆W to the original weight matrix W such that the compressed streams reproduce the full-width outputs as closely as possible. 4 Experiments 

Setup. We use the original SliceGPT Ashkboos et al. [2024] as our baseline and evaluate three additional variants: a PCA-only configuration, corresponding to SliceGPT without pruning; Ex-panded Calibration Dataset (ECD), which augments SliceGPT with the expanded calibration dataset introduced above; and our full method, I REKO GPT, which combines ECD with the ridge-based correction described in Sec. 3.3. We evaluate four models: Llama-2-7B Touvron et al. [2023], Llama-3.2-1B Meta AI [2024], Llama-3.1-8B Grattafiori et al. [2024], and Qwen3-1.7B Yang et al. [2025]. The calibration set consists of 128 sequences of length 2048 sampled from the WikiText-2 training split Merity et al. [2017]. For ECD, we use the same set of compression ratios across all models, R = {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. For the ridge-based correction in I REKO GPT, we target the value projection WV , which showed promising performance in preliminary experiments. To reduce VRAM usage, at each layer we randomly sample B = 65 ,536 tokens ( 25% of the calibration tokens) for the regression step, and set λ = 1 in all experiments. All experiments are run on cluster nodes equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 64 GB of VRAM. 

Evaluation. Following SliceGPT Ashkboos et al. [2024], We evaluate all methods on language modeling by measuring perplexity on WikiText-2 Merity et al. [2017]. We use a maximum sequence length of 2048 , truncating longer examples and concatenating shorter ones to match the target length. Tab. 1 presents the main results. Notably, the 25% and 35% compression ratios are never included in the expanded calibration set, and therefore also assess generalization to unseen widths. Its performance remains reasonably close to SliceGPT, confirming that this construction already provides a viable slimmable model. Building on this baseline, ECD consistently improves performance and substantially reduces the gap with SliceGPT across models and sparsity levels, validating the benefit of calibrating over multiple widths. The gap becomes especially evident as sparsity increases, supporting our hypothesis that correcting compression-induced representation shifts is key to preserving the original model behavior. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced I REKO GPT, a post-hoc approach for turning pretrained LLMs into slimmable models by reinterpreting SliceGPT’s PCA structure. By calibrating across multiple widths and leveraging full-width signals, I REKO GPT improves robustness across compression levels while retaining a single dynamically configurable model. 4References
