# Abstract 

Activation sparsity speeds up large language model (LLM) inference by setting unimportant activations to zero so that the corresponding computations can be skipped. Existing training-free methods, however, make different trade-offs: threshold-based methods such as TEAL adapt the sparsity level to each token but do not tightly control the realised sparsity, while TopK-based methods such as WINA enforce a fixed sparsity level but use the same sparsity budget for every token. Both also apply the same budget across transformer blocks, despite large dif-ferences in block sensitivity. We introduce TopK-Guided , a training-free method that addresses both limitations by combining bounded token-level sparsity adap-tation with sensitivity-aware block-level budget allocation. Across Llama-2 and Llama-3 models, TopK-Guided consistently improves perplexity and downstream accuracy over TEAL and WINA while preserving essentially the same sparsity-dependent projection compute as WINA, with the largest gains at high sparsity. Ablations show that both components provide complementary improvements. 

# 1 Introduction 

Every token generated by a large language model (LLM) requires a full forward pass through the network, and within each transformer block most of this cost comes from a few large linear projections ( Wq , W k, W v , W o in attention and Wgate , W up , W down in the MLP), each multiplying a weight matrix by an activation vector [14]. A well-documented property of trained LLMs is that many of the activation entries feeding these projections are already close to zero at inference time [12] and contribute negligibly to the output [14, 15]. Activation sparsity exploits this. Before a projection W h , it sets the smallest-magnitude entries of the activation vector h to zero; whenever an entry hj is zeroed, the corresponding column W:,j can be skipped, since it only ever multiplies hj . Zeroing a fraction s of the entries thus skips a fraction 

s of the projection’s work. Unlike weight pruning [19, 7], which permanently removes the same parameters for every input, activation sparsity leaves the model untouched and chooses its sparsity pattern afresh for each token [14]. Given a target sparsity s, a method must decide, for each token, which entries of h to zero. Two recent methods define the state of the art and adopt opposite strategies. TEAL [13] calibrates, per projection, a single threshold τ offline so that on average a fraction s of activation magnitudes fall below it, and at inference retains every entry above the threshold:

WINA [3] takes the opposite strategy: rather than fixing a threshold and letting the number of kept entries vary, it fixes that number directly through TopK selection. Since k is the same for every token, exactly a fraction s is zeroed each time, so the realised sparsity always matches the target. But fixing k removes the token adaptivity that TEAL had for free. The two methods therefore sit at opposite ends of a single trade-off. TEAL adapts to each token but cannot control the realised sparsity, while WINA controls the sparsity exactly but cannot adapt to the token. Ideally we would want both: a mask that adjusts to each token while keeping the realised sparsity close to the target. Both TEAL and WINA share a further limitation: they assign the same budget s to every transformer block. Blocks, however, are not equally tolerant of sparsification: sparsifying each block on its own and measuring how much its output changes shows that early blocks are far more sensitive than later ones (Figure 1). A uniform budget therefore over-sparsifies the fragile early blocks and under-uses the robust later ones. We propose TopK-Guided , two complementary training-free modifications to WINA, one for each issue: 1) TopK-Guided masking (Section 2.1) recovers the per-token adaptivity that WINA gives up while keeping the realised sparsity within a bounded ±δ of the target. We reuse TEAL’s calibrated threshold τ to estimate how sparse each token naturally is, then clip that estimate to a narrow band 

±δ around s, before applying TopK. 2) Sensitivity-aware block sparsity (Section 2.2) replaces the uniform budget with one matched to each block’s sensitivity: a single calibration pass measures it, and sensitive blocks then get less, robust ones more. Like OWL[22], we allocate sparsity non-uniformly across blocks, but base it on a direct sensitivity measurement rather than a weight-outlier signal. Across Llama-2-7B [20] and Llama-3-8B [9], TopK-Guided beats both TEAL and WINA on perplex-ity and downstream tasks at matched compute, with the largest gains at high sparsity. 

# 2 Method 

We build our method on top of WINA, keeping its tensor rotation of the residual stream (Appendix C) and its greedy per-projection allocation within each block (Appendix D) unchanged; with sparsifi-cation applied only during the decode phase. Our two contributions modify WINA in exactly two places: the masking rule and the block-level budget assignment. So at matched compute it improves on WINA on every setting, while also improving on threshold masking, whose realised sparsity can fall short of the target and deliver less than the promised saving, where our bounded deviation guarantees the target reduction is met. And it comes for free architecturally too: TopK-Guided operates on the same SVD-rotated weights WINA inherits from SliceGPT [1], the basis in which rotation-based quantization such as QuaRot [2] also acts, so the two compose on the same computationally-invariant model with no extra machinery. 

# 4 Conclusion and Limitations 

We presented TopK-Guided, two training-free changes to WINA that add no cost over it yet con-sistently improve on both WINA and TEAL. TopK-Guided masking recovers per-token adaptivity while keeping the realised sparsity within a bounded ±δ of the target, and sensitivity-aware block sparsity splits a fixed global budget across blocks by a single-pass sensitivity estimate. The gains hold across both models and all sparsities and are largest where sparsification is most aggressive, and an ablation confirms that each contribution helps on its own. Our study has two main limits. We evaluate decoder-only models up to 8B parameters, so the method’s behaviour at larger scale and on other architectures remains to be checked, though it makes no architecture-specific assumptions. We also report FLOP-level savings rather than wall-clock speedup, leaving a kernel-level implementation to future work. Finally, because TopK-Guided operates on the same rotated, computationally-invariant model as recent rotation-based quantization, combining the two is a natural next step toward faster low-precision inference. 4References

# B Target Sparsity Vs Realised Sparsity 

Threshold masking calibrates a fixed threshold τ offline to hit a target sparsity s, but because τ is frozen after calibration while each token’s activation distribution differs, the fraction of activations actually falling below τ at inference varies from token to token and need not average to s. TopK masking, by retaining exactly k = ⌊d(1 − s)⌋ elements per token, hits the target by construction. Table 3 quantifies this on Llama-2-7B at a target of s = 0 .5 across three datasets. TEAL’s threshold masking consistently under-shoots the target, and by an amount that drifts from one dataset to the next, so its realised sparsity, and hence its actual speedup, is neither on target nor predictable. WINA’s TopK hits the target exactly on every dataset, by construction. TopK-Guided stays within its ±δ band of the target ( δ = 0 .01 ) on all three datasets: it recovers threshold masking’s per-token adaptivity while keeping the realised sparsity controlled, close to the target and stable across datasets, which is exactly what neither baseline achieves at once. 7C Tensor Rotation: What, Why, and How 

Both WINA and our TopK-Guided method operate on a rotated version of the model’s weights and activations. This appendix explains what the rotation is, why it makes WINA’s weight-informed score well-founded, and how it is applied at zero inference cost.

If the columns of W were orthogonal, then ⟨W:,j , W :,k ⟩ = 0 for j̸ = k, the cross-terms in Equation 7 would vanish, and the per-element score would sum to the true error. WINA achieves this, following SliceGPT [1], by right-multiplying each weight matrix with an orthogonal matrix Q obtained from its own Singular Value Decomposition. Writing W = U ΣV ⊤

This rotation is data-dependent, derived from an actual weight matrix rather than fixed in advance. The SVD construction yields exact orthogonality for the matrix whose SVD is taken, at the cost of requiring the propagation machinery below to preserve the model’s output. 

C.3 The Implementation: Computational Invariance 

Orthogonalising a standalone layer y = W h via ˆW = W Q would change its output unless the input is also transformed to Q⊤h. In a transformer, that input transformation must be produced by the preceding layer, which then requires its own compensation, and so on. SliceGPT’s key observation is that this chain closes: the required rotations can be absorbed into adjacent weight matrices throughout the network, so no rotation is ever computed at runtime. RMSNorm is rotation-equivariant and residual connections are linear, so both commute cleanly with Q.8After this one-time offline preprocessing, the rotated model produces exactly the same outputs as the original (the computational invariance property of SliceGPT) while performing the same number of FLOPs at inference. All experiments in this paper, including sensitivity estimation and TopK-Guided masking, operate on these rotated weights. 

# D Block-wise Greedy Sparsity Allocation 

Both TEAL and WINA, and our method, allocate a block’s sparsity budget non-uniformly across its weight matrices using a greedy search. This appendix describes that procedure, which our sensitivity-aware budgets feed into unchanged. 

Intuition: Within a transformer block there are n weight matrices ( Wq , W k, W v , W o in attention and Wgate , W up , W down in the MLP). They are not equally forgiving under sparsification: zeroing activations feeding into some matrices barely changes the block’s output, while the same fraction into others causes large error. The greedy algorithm exploits this asymmetry. It repeatedly asks which matrix can absorb a little more sparsity for the smallest increase in output error, and assigns that matrix the next increment. Repeating this traces out a good allocation for every overall budget, computed once at calibration and looked up at inference.

against the dense block output Ygt , then undo the change; (ii) permanently commit the increment for the matrix j = arg min i Ei with the least error; (iii) recompute P and record the row (P, p 1, . . . , p n)

in the lookup table. The full procedure is given in Algorithm 1. 

Worked example: Consider a toy block with two matrices: a small matrix A with fA = 100 

and a large matrix B with fB = 400 (so F = 500 ), using α = 0 .1. The step sizes are δA =0.1 · 500 /100 = 0 .5 and δB = 0 .1 · 500 /400 = 0 .125 , so A moves in large jumps and B in small ones. Suppose the errors evolve as in Table 4. At steps 1 and 2, sparsifying B by its small step costs far less than sparsifying A by its large one, so B is chosen. By step 3, B has absorbed two increments and its next one costs more than giving A its first, so the algorithm switches to A. After three steps the block reaches P = 0 .3 with the non-uniform allocation pA = 0 .5, p B = 0 .25 , an allocation a uniform “sparsify everything by 30%” baseline would not find. Over the real n = 7 matrices, the large MLP matrices tend to absorb sparsity earliest, while smaller, more sensitive attention projections are protected until later. # H Impact Statement 

This work aims to reduce the computational cost of LLM inference, which may lower energy use and make LLM deployment more accessible in resource-constrained settings. We do not identify any direct negative societal impacts specific to the proposed method, as it does not introduce new model capabilities, training data, or user-facing behavior. 12
