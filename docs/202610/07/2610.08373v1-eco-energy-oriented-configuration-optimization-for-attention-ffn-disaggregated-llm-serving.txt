ABSTRACT 

Energy-efficient LLM serving requires minimizing serving GPU energy while meeting latency and throughput service-level objectives (SLOs). Attention–FFN disaggregation (AFD) enables separate resource allocation and operating con-trols for attention and expert computation, but their energy effects remain cou-pled through the execution pipeline. Realizing its energy-saving potential there-fore requires navigating a hierarchical configuration space in which deployment structures constrain admissible controls and shape their end-to-end effects. Find-ing low-energy configurations that meet SLOs is challenging because physical evaluations are costly and only a small fraction of candidates can be measured. We present Energy-Oriented Configuration Optimization (ECO), which jointly searches deployment structures and their admissible operating controls under a limited measurement budget. ECO constructs a structure-aware energy prior from calibrated stage behavior and pipeline dependencies, then learns residual predic-tion errors with a Gaussian process. Its cost-aware constrained Bayesian optimiza-tion prioritizes measurements according to expected energy improvement while accounting for SLO feasibility, execution success, and evaluation cost, and returns the lowest-energy measured feasible configuration. Across all 16 scenarios on A6000 and A100 with Qwen and DeepSeek, ECO’s frozen configurations, eval-uated on disjoint requests, reduce serving energy by 40.5% and increase output token rate by 20.7% on average relative to baselines while meeting target SLOs. Across the 8 A6000 scenarios, its selected feasible energy averages 33.1% below generic constrained Bayesian optimization and 25.8% below genetic search. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Energy-efficient LLM serving requires minimizing serving GPU energy while meeting latency and throughput service-level objectives (SLOs). Attention–feed-forward network (FFN) disaggregation (AFD) provides additional flexibility for pursuing this objective. Attention and expert FFNs have different computation, memory, and communication demands (Rajbhandari et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2025). By placing them on separate GPU groups, AFD allows each role to use its own resource allocation, parallelism, frequency, and power cap (Zhu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). By placing them on separate GPU groups, AFD provides flexibility in both the deployment structure , including GPU allocation, parallelism, and dual batch overlap (DBO) settings, and the operating controls ,including per-role frequencies and power caps (Zhu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). The central configuration problem is how to use this flexibility to reduce energy across the active GPUs while satisfying fixed service requirements. Three properties of AFD make this problem challenging. 

First , deployment structure and operating controls are coupled through the execution pipeline. Each microbatch passes through attention, dispatch, FFN, and combine before entering the next layer. Slowing one stage may reduce its power draw, but can also delay dependent stages and extend the time during which other GPUs consume power. A local power reduction therefore need not reduce total serving energy: its benefit depends on how the selected structure permits work to overlap and delays to propagate. Second , AFD has a hierarchical configuration space. GPU allocation constrains the parallelism choices available to each role, while parallelism and DBO settings determine how work is partitioned and overlapped within the deployment. Frequencies and power caps must also satisfy the constraints of the assigned hardware. These dependencies restrict which combinations of settings form valid configurations and must be respected during search. However, satisfying the known static constraints does not ensure that a configuration will execute successfully or meet the SLOs. 

Third , configuration selection depends on the model and offered load. The same deployment struc-ture and operating controls can produce different energy and service responses across models and workloads. As load increases, a configuration may need higher operating frequencies, additional GPUs, or a different allocation between attention and FFN computation to remain feasible. The appropriate adjustment can differ across models, so deployment structures and operating controls need to be evaluated jointly for the target model and workload. Identifying suitable configurations under these conditions requires extensive measurements, but each measurement is costly. Each trial incurs lifecycle overhead from deployment, warmup, workload re-play, and telemetry collection, and failed trials consume budget too. Our audited A6000 spaces con-tain 2,700–22,950 statically legal candidates, while 16 search attempts cover only 0.070–0.593%. Such sparse coverage makes it difficult to learn the energy and feasibility of unmeasured configu-rations solely from observed outcomes. The practical problem is therefore to identify low-energy, SLO-feasible configurations in a structured search space using a limited measurement budget. Prior systems establish the value of coordinated provisioning and frequency control. AFD systems study resource partitioning and communication-aware execution (Zhu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026); DynamoLLM coordinates instance count, parallelism, and frequency (Stojkovic et al., 2024); and AFlex combines AFD provisioning with operator profiling and GPU frequency control (Hu et al., 2026). Our focus is how to select energy-efficient, SLO-feasible configurations when measurements for the target deployment are limited. A black-box energy surrogate without pipeline information must learn configuration effects primarily from sparse observations. AFD, however, exposes use-ful structure before search begins: configuration rules constrain legal deployments, and pipeline dependencies shape how operating controls affect energy. The key question is how to incorporate this information into an energy prior that helps identify promising feasible configurations within the available measurement budget. We present Energy-Oriented Configuration Optimization (ECO) to address this question. To our knowledge, ECO is the first design-space exploration framework jointly searching AFD struc-tures and GPU frequencies under a limited measurement budget. As search proceeds, stage records from measured configurations calibrate structure-specific stage behavior. ECO propagates predicted stage times through pipeline dependencies and combines the resulting timing estimates with cali-brated GPU power to estimate serving energy. A Gaussian process (GP) learns residual prediction errors from completed trials, refining the prior with measured outcomes. Building on constrained and cost-aware Bayesian optimization (Gelbart et al., 2014; Gardner et al., 2014; Snoek et al., 2012), ECO’s cost-aware constrained Bayesian optimization (C 2BO) prioritizes candidates according to ex-pected energy improvement while accounting for SLO feasibility, execution success, and evaluation cost. Acquisition-based choices are interleaved with structure and single-control probes that sup-ply calibration data within the same search allowance. ECO returns the lowest-energy measured configuration that passes the execution and SLO eligibility checks, with its settings fixed during subsequent serving. 2 AFD C ONFIGURATION LANDSCAPE 

AFD places attention and expert FFN computation on separate GPU groups. Microbatches follow the attention–dispatch–FFN–combine pipeline across layers and can overlap when resources permit (Figure 1). This section examines how deployment structures and operating controls interact through the pipeline, how configuration choices depend on one another, and how model and load affect configuration selection under fixed SLOs. 

Motivation 1: Structure–control coupling. A role’s frequency affects both its power and the timing of dependent stages. Slowing attention can delay FFN execution, while slowing FFN can delay the next layer. A local power reduction can therefore extend the time during which other GPUs consume power, so its effect on total serving energy depends on how delays propagate through the pipeline. DBO thresholds control when two microbatches overlap, and overlap and shared resources shape this propagation (Yu et al., 2022; Agrawal et al., 2024). Consequently, isolated operator energy profiles do not capture the full serving response. The matched Qwen measurements at 16 requests/s in Figure 2 illustrate this dependence. With other settings fixed, raising the FFN clock target from 2010 to 2100 MHz increases energy under DBO thresholds (32 , 512) but decreases it under (64 , 1024) . The difference between these two energy changes is −1.303 kJ (Appendix D). Each cell contains one observation, however, and three cells violate the throughput SLO; repeated measurements are needed to distinguish the interaction from run-to-run variation. We use this local reversal as motivation for evaluating operating controls within their deployment structure. 

Motivation 2: Hierarchical configuration space. GPU allocation constrains the parallelism avail-able to each role. Let NA and NF denote the numbers of attention and FFN GPUs. Tensor par-allelism (TP) partitions computation, data parallelism (DP) distributes request batches, and expert parallelism (EP) distributes experts. In our backend, TP r must divide Nr , DP r = Nr /TP r for 

r ∈ { A, F }, and EP F = NF ; allocation additionally requires NA ≥ NF and NF | NA. For example, 4A+2F admits attention TP degrees {1, 2, 4} and FFN TP degrees {1, 2} before model and memory checks. Energy optimization must therefore respect the dependencies that define valid deployment structures and evaluate operating controls within those structures. 4 ECO M ETHODS 

ECO calibrates structure-specific stage responses, propagates timing through the pipeline, and learns residual energy error (Figure 3). After candidate validation and a reference measurement, C 2BO updates its predictive models to select each trial. All trials use the same fixed search workload. We reserve calibration for fitting ECO’s stage responses within the search budget. ECO returns the lowest-energy measured feasible configuration. 4.1 PREDICTIVE MODELS 

The energy surrogate combines a calibrated stage-and-pipeline prior with GP residual correction. Calibration fits each deployment structure s separately because stage responses to operating controls 

u are structure dependent. 

Stage-time model. We decompose each layer into Attention ( A), dispatch ( D), FFN ( F ), and combine ( C). Within each structure, nonnegative least squares fits stage duration to prefill and decode token counts. Paired measurements that vary one operating control estimate its effect on stage time. Multiple changes combine these local responses unless calibration identifies a joint effect; unidentified effects and extrapolation increase prior uncertainty. Appendix B.2 gives the fitting equations and implementation details. 

Pipeline and energy model. First-in, first-out (FIFO) simulator propagates predicted stage times through a finite number of in-flight microbatches, capturing execution time. An operation starts when its predecessor has completed and its resource is free: Fo = max {Fpred( o), A res( o)} + bso,where Fo is operation o’s finish time and Ares( o) is its resource’s availability time. This recurrence propagates local timing changes through dependencies and resource contention. A microbatch enters the next layer only after combine, and two communication directions share resource by default. The completion time bTpipe gives the capacity estimate bC = Nref / bTpipe , where Nref is the calibrated number of requests per pipeline unit. Filter candidates and schedule measurements. C2BO removes configurations that violate static constraints; for example, 3A+2F violates NF | NA. Execution success must still be established by measurement. After the reference, C 2BO interleaves acquisition choices with untried structures and single-control probes, such as varying only the Attention clock to estimate its stage-time response. Rank candidates and select the final configuration. C2BO defers candidates whose optimistic predictions violate an SLO or exceed the best measured feasible energy by a margin, then measures the remaining candidate with the highest acquisition score (Equation 3). 5 EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

We evaluate Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026) and DeepSeek-V2-Lite-Chat (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) using vLLM 0.26.0 (Kwon et al., 2023) with AFD on A100-SXM4 (80 GB) and A6000 (48 GB) GPUs. On each platform, both models use four-GPU pools at 4/8 requests/s and eight-GPU pools at 8/16 requests/s. ECO, constrained BO, and GA independently search the same fixed 200-request workload constructed from the Splitwise Azure trace (Patel et al., 2024b). For each sce-nario, we repeat each method’s search with multiple random seeds and report averages across these independent runs. Each run allows 16 physical search trials, including initialization probes and fail-ures, plus one shared preparation evaluation charged separately (17 evaluations per run in total). Search and held-out replays use real text prompts from replay files; request identities, prompts, to-ken lengths, and arrival schedules are identical across methods within each split. BO uses a constant energy prior. Default uses the full GPU pool with equal Attention and FFN groups, while selected configurations may use fewer GPUs. SLO thresholds are fixed before search at 105% of historical Default latency and 95% of historical Default throughput, serving as latency ceilings and through-put floors, respectively. TTBT constraints apply where specified, and all thresholds remain fixed during held-out evaluation. In each run, each method freezes its lowest-energy measured feasible configuration before replaying 200 disjoint held-out requests. Default is rerun on the same held-out requests and arrival schedule to provide the comparison denominators. Figures 4 and 5 report p90 TTFT/TPOT, output tokens/s, and serving energy from these replays; held-out results do not affect configuration selection. Appendix A details the measurement protocol, and Appendix B.5 specifies BO and GA settings. 5.1 RESULTS

The full surrogate finds lower-energy configurations. All three ablations use DeepSeek on four A100 GPUs. Full ECO finds the lowest feasible energy at every budget in Figure 7 (left). At budget 16, Full ECO uses 10.1% less energy than Constant prior + GP, which overtakes Mechanism prior only; Simplified pipeline + GP remains 9.2% above Full ECO. These variants respectively replace the mechanism prior with a constant, remove residual correction, or replace FIFO scheduling with a bottleneck approximation. The trends suggest that the prior guides early search, residual learning corrects persistent error, and pipeline scheduling improves energy estimates. 

Joint tuning yields additional energy savings. Joint tuning saves 40.2% and 18.1% relative to each load’s Default at 4 and 8 requests/s in Figure 7 (middle), compared with 30.7% and 14.6% for se-quential tuning. The additional energy reduction over sequential tuning narrows from 13.7% to 4.1% at higher load. Sequential tuning fixes the deployment structure before adjusting operating controls, restricting the combinations considered in the second step; joint tuning retains the alternatives.

6 RELATED WORK 

Power and energy management. POLCA supports cluster power oversubscription (Patel et al., 2024a); DynamoLLM jointly adjusts instance count, parallelism, and frequency (Stojkovic et al., 2024); DualScale coordinates placement and frequency control in prefill/decode disaggregation (Ba-sit et al., 2026). AFlex is closest to our setting, combining Attention–FFN provisioning and fre-quency control with offline operator profiles, global scheduling, and adaptive pipelining and batch-ing (Hu et al., 2026). Runtime approaches include throttLL’eM’s iteration-level performance predic-tion (Kakolyris et al., 2024) and GreenLLM’s separate prefill optimization and decode feedback con-trol (Liu et al., 2025). ECO uses AFD hierarchy and pipeline dependencies to select measurements under a limited search budget, returning a measured static configuration for the target workload. 

Automatic configuration search. CherryPick uses BO for cloud configuration selection (Alipour-fard et al., 2017). For LLM inference, SCOOT combines single- and multi-objective BO, known-constraint pruning, hidden-constraint modeling, and parallel suggestions (Cheng et al., 2025). ECO builds on constrained BO for unknown feasibility (Gelbart et al., 2014; Gardner et al., 2014) and cost-aware BO for varying evaluation duration (Snoek et al., 2012). Its AFD-specific energy prior conditions stage responses on deployment structure and propagates pipeline timing before residual correction. Our characterization and model ablation evaluate the information this prior supplies. 

7 CONCLUSION 

ECO combines a calibrated stage-and-pipeline energy prior, residual learning, and cost-aware con-strained Bayesian optimization to jointly select AFD deployment structures and operating controls under a limited measurement budget. Across 16 A6000 and A100 scenarios, frozen configurations evaluated on disjoint requests reduce serving energy by 40.5% and increase output token rate by 20.7% on average relative to Default while meeting SLOs. Future work should evaluate separate adaptation schedules end-to-end and quantify their mechanisms through controlled attribution and stage-waiting measurements. 9Preprint

GA: selection, variation, and repair. Each parent is selected by a size-two tournament sampled without replacement; the second tournament excludes the first parent. With probability 0.9, uniform crossover independently chooses each gene from either parent with equal probability; otherwise the child copies the first parent. Each gene then mutates independently with probability 0.2 to a uniformly chosen alternative value present in the legal space. The child is repaired to an untried affordable legal candidate with minimum gene-wise Hamming distance, with uniform random tie-breaking. Repair uses configuration distance, not predicted fitness of unmeasured candidates. Each step reinitializes Python’s random generator with the string ga-v1:0: t, where t is the number of previous search attempts.
