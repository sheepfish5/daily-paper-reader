## Abstract 

We study Bayesian optimization (BO) through the lens of information geometry. Pulling back the Fisher information metric through the surrogate posterior map yields a local sensitivity tensor on the input space, which leads to an upper bound on the gradient of reparameterizable acquisition functions. This view explains vanishing-gradient behavior in high-dimensional BO and provides a common interpretation of heuristics such as RAASP and dimension-scaled lengthscales. Building on this analysis, we propose FITR, a trust-region-based BO method that replaces lengthscale-based scaling by local pullback-Fisher weights. FITR is not restricted to GP kernels with explicit lengthscales. On GP benchmarks with an SE kernel, experiments show competitive performance using FITR. The proposed method also easily generalizes to non-isotropic surrogates although the gains are more task-dependent in that setting.

Bayesian Optimization (BO) is a popular framework for optimizing black-box functions with ex-pensive evaluations [2]. It has proven to be effective in hyperparameter tuning [3], robotics policy 

Preprint. arXiv:2609.31107v1 [cs.LG] 25 Sep 2026 search [4], and engineering design [5]. As explained in Section 2, BO alternates between fitting a probabilistic surrogate Mt to the observed data Dt and optimizing an acquisition function (AF) α(·)

to select the next input candidate xt+1 . In practice, the inner AF optimization is often non-trivial. The AF is usually non-convex, depends on the surrogate through a predictive distribution, and becomes increasingly difficult to optimize as the input dimension Dx grows. This issue is now well documented in the high-dimensional BO (HDBO) literature. Recent work shows that both surrogate training and AF optimization can suffer from vanishing gradients in large dimensions [6–8]. Proposed fixes include dimensionally scaled lengthscale priors [6, 7], Random Axis Aligned Subspace Perturbations (RAASP) initializations [9], conformal mappings [10], and trust-region-based search [11]. A central question remains: what common mechanism makes AF optimization collapse, and how much of that mechanism is tied to the surrogate model? Inspired by the work of Herrmann et al. [12], we analyze BO from a different perspective. As shown in Figure 1, instead of looking at the AF only as a function over X , we utilize the chain rule and information geometry. The surrogate Mt is a map from input space X to the statistical manifold S

of predictive distributions. These predictive distribution parameters, like mean and variance, are the parameters that the AF really depends on. Information geometry [13, 14] becomes a natural way to study how the surrogate induces geometry on the input space through the pullback Fisher tensor. This induced geometry tells us how strongly local perturbations in x change the predictive distribution. This view separates two effects that are usually entangled in AF optimization. The acquisition design determines how sensitive the utility is to changes in the predictive distribution. The surrogate-induced geometry determines whether moving in input space produces such changes at all. Section 4 formalizes this separation through an acquisition-gradient bound. This perspective then becomes a common analytical tool for understanding HDBO: • In Section 4, we analyze the pullback geometry induced by the surrogate posterior map and derive a bound for the AF gradient norm ∥∇ xα∥2 that separates acquisition-side sensitivity from surrogate-induced geometry. • We use this factorization to analyze vanishing-gradient behavior in HDBO under a common framework. This yields a unified interpretation of dimensionally scaled priors [6, 7] and RAASP [8, 9]. • In Section 5, we propose the Fisher-Information Trust Region (FITR), which replaces kernel-lengthscale scaling by local pullback-Fisher weights. FITR applies to differentiable probabilistic surrogates that satisfy the stated regularity conditions and is not restricted to GP kernels with explicit lengthscales. Section 6 shows that FITR improves on TuRBO-REI on several benchmarks and remains competitive with strong baselines on the remainder.

Surrogate Models At each iteration t, the surrogate model Mt models the predictive distribution 

p(y|x, Dt) = p(y; θt, x), where θt, x ∈ S are the parameters of the predictive distribution [17]. The hyperparameters λt of Mt specify the distribution parameters θt, x ← M t(x; Dt) and can be trained by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood [18] or another suitable objective [19, 20]. θt, x on the statistical manifold S, the set of all distributions the surrogate can produce [13]. Input locations that appear far apart in Euclidean space may be close in the manifold view because the surrogate induces its own local geometry, as shown by the +++ and ××× markers in Figure 1. This map is at least C1 differentiable, a mild condition satisfied by many popular surrogate models such as 2Gaussian processes (GPs) with differentiable kernels [18] or Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) with smooth activations [21]. ## 3 Related Work 

Information geometry and applications. Natural gradient descent (NGD) is a key application of this theory [25, 26]. Work by Khan and Rue [27] shows how algorithms such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and Kalman filters can be derived from an algorithm called Bayesian Learning Rule ,and how natural gradients can improve the resulting updates. As another application of information geometry, Kim et al. [28] presented Fisher-Sharpness-Aware Minimization (FSAM), which replaced the Euclidean ball in sharpness-aware minimization with a Fisher ellipsoid, improving generalization and robustness. Beik-Mohammadi et al. [29] learn Riemannian manifolds for geodesic motion generation in robotics. 

Geometry-aware Bayesian optimization. Jaquier et al. [4], Jaquier and Rozo [30] introduced geometry-aware kernels that used geodesic distances in Bayesian optimization when the search space is non-Euclidean, such as robot orientation. However, in our work, we explore the geometry that is induced in the search space by the posterior map of the surrogate model Mt as mentioned in (1). We do not assume that the search space has any intrinsic geometric structure. Recent work by Yu et al. 3[31] proposes GIT-BO with Tabular Foundation Model (TabPFNv2) as the surrogate model. They use the gradient of the predictive mean to identify a global active subspace for high-dimensional BO. Our use of Fisher geometry is local and trust-region based: we shape candidate regions around the current center using the predictive distribution, not only the mean. A direct comparison with GIT-BO would therefore mix surrogate choice with trust-region geometry. 

Pullback metrics for probabilistic models. Tosi et al. [32] proposed the idea of pulling back a Riemannian metric for Gaussian Process Latent Variable Models (GPLVMs). They derived the expected metric tensor and used the resulting geodesics for improving data interpolation. Arvanitidis et al. [33] showed how the latent space can be characterized by a stochastic Riemannian metric, resulting in improved sampling and interpolation in latent space. Arvanitidis et al. [34] used the Fisher-Rao metric associated with the space of decoder distributions, enabling meaningful latent geometries for a broader class of decoders. Rozo et al. [35] generalized the construction to wrapped GPLVMs defined on Riemannian data manifolds, where they pull back a Riemannian structure from the data manifold to the latent space. 

High-dimensional Bayesian optimization. Recent work by Hvarfner et al. [6], Xu et al. [7] has shown that BO can be effective in high dimensions (HDBO). They analyze the vanishing-gradient problem in surrogate-model and acquisition-function (AF) optimization in high dimensions. They propose a dimensionally scaled lengthscale prior to avoid the vanishing gradient problem. Papenmeier et al. [8] confirm the finding and propose √Dx/10 initialization of lengthscales for Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) for GP surrogates. They also show that RAASP for AF optimization helps in preventing vanishing gradients. Local search for global optimization and trust-region methods offer complementary approaches. TuRBO is a popular trust-region-based method proposed by Eriksson et al. [11] to address high di-mensionality. They use local axis-aligned hyperrectangles to define the trust region around the current best point. Work by Papenmeier et al. [9, 36] extends trust-region-based methods to combinatorial and mixed spaces as well. However, existing trust-region constructions in this setting rely on GP surrogates with explicit lengthscale parameters like squared exponential kernel or Matern kernel. 

## 4 Manifold View of Bayesian Optimization 

As mentioned in Section 2, the acquisition function depends on x through the surrogate’s predictive parameters θt, x. Viewing the surrogate as a smooth map φt : X → S into the statistical manifold of predictive distributions shifts the analysis of acquisition-gradient behavior to the geometry of this map. Pulling back the Fisher–Rao metric from S to X via φt yields the pullback Fisher tensor gt(x),which quantifies how sensitively p(y; θt, x) changes under local perturbations of the input. We define the pullback Fisher tensor and establish an acquisition gradient bound (Section 4.1). Then in Section 4.2 we show how the critical λ scaling of √Dx from [6–8] and other HDBO findings like RAASP can be viewed from the geometric perspective of the pullback Fisher tensor.

TuRBO connection Figure 2 shows that the pullback trace and the AF gradient remain non-degenerate near the observed data. TuRBO devel-ops on a similar principle, where it centers a local axis-aligned TR at the incumbent and adapts its size over time using the learned lengthscales [11]. The dimensions with longer lengthscales have a longer TR length. Appendix D.6 shows that the metric tensor at a local optimum xbest is often dominated by the variance-gradient term, which is inversely proportional to the lengthscales. The ellipsoid made using the pullback metric would be longer in the dimensions with longer lengthscales. This is similar to how TuRBO uses the learned lengthscales to create TR. This motivates the use of pullback-metric-based TR for BO, which we call the Fisher-Information Trust Region (FITR). The FITR is a location-dependent TR that can be extended to any surrogate for which the derivative of the predictive distribution with respect to the input variables can be calculated. 

## 5 Fisher-Information Trust Region (FITR) 

As explained in Appendix B.1, TuRBO centers the trust region at the best observed location xbest and scales the dimension-wise side lengths using kernel lengthscales [11]. This construction is effective because it avoids performing the inner AF optimization in large regions where the surrogate has little information. Since the kernels are isotropic, the notion of distance underlying TuRBO remains Euclidean. TuRBO has proven effective in practice, but is also limited to SE or Matérn kernels. Section 4 suggests a different local notion of distance. The acquisition depends on x through φt(x),so the relevant local geometry is defined by the pullback Fisher tensor gt(x) in (5). This tensor can be computed for any differentiable probabilistic surrogate that satisfies Assumption 4.1. The ideal local Fisher region is the ellipsoid

Limitations. Appendix D.7 shows that the FIM is the second-order Taylor expansion of Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence [13, 24]. A purely KL-derived trust region is inherently mode-seeking 6and can become too exploitative near local optima [37]. We therefore retain TuRBO’s outer trust-region scaling rule, Algorithm 3, to preserve its exploration behavior. A second issue is scalability with Dx, since the full local ellipsoid requires inversion or whitening of the pullback tensor. For scalar predictive families, the pullback rank is at most two, so the tensor is rank-deficient when 

Dx > 2. Without regularization, near-null directions would produce arbitrarily large radii. Popular BO frameworks such as BoTorch also largely support sampling from axis-aligned trust regions. One can create a hyperrectangle from the pullback tensor by using the infinity norm and whitening to make it axis-aligned. However, once such a region intersects the constraints/bounds of X , the feasible set becomes a clipped polytope rather than a simple box. Polytope sampling is substantially slower and less efficient than simple axis-aligned Sobol sampling for multi-start AF optimization.

FITR improves on the non-trust-region baseline on a subset of tasks and remains comparable on the remainder. These results show that the same FITR pipeline can be paired with a non-lengthscale kernel, although the gains for anisotropic kernels in trust-region methods appear task-dependent. 

## 7 Limitations 

Proposition 4.2 bounds the acquisition gradient during inner optimization; it does not imply a regret guarantee or a global convergence statement for the outer BO loop. One could also replace the trust region by a KL-divergence constraint and solve the resulting problem with an Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM) [39]. During our experiments, however, this ALM-based variant became highly exploitative and less exploratory, which degraded performance. For non-isotropic kernels like IBNNs, the pullback trace need not collapse with Dx in the same way as for many lengthscale-dominated GPs [21], so the vanishing-gradient narrative from Section 4.2 applies less sharply. The improvement in performance using FITR becomes task-dependent. A precise analysis of when local Fisher geometry predicts a measurable search advantage beyond isotropic or deep-kernel setups is left for future work. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We propose an information-geometric perspective on Bayesian optimization (BO). The surrogate is treated as a smooth map from the input space to the manifold of predictive distributions, and reparameterizable acquisition functions depend on the input only through these predictive parameters. Proposition 4.2 separates the norm of the acquisition gradient into two factors: an acquisition-side sensitivity and the trace of the pullback Fisher tensor induced by the surrogate. For bounded acquisition-side sensitivity, the acquisition gradient is vanished when the pullback trace collapses. This provides a common explanation for several empirical observations in high-dimensional BO, including dimension-scaled lengthscales and local initialization around observed data. Using these insights, we propose the Fisher-Information Trust Region (FITR), a simple trust-region method for differentiable probabilistic surrogates that satisfy the stated regularity assumptions. FITR shapes the trust region using local pullback-Fisher weights at the current center and replaces the lengthscale-based scaling in TuRBO by a diagonal approximation to the regularized pullback Fisher tensor. In our experiments, this diagonal FITR variant improves on TuRBO-REI on several GP benchmarks and remains competitive elsewhere. FITR does not introduce task-specific tuning and the same construction can be extended to other predictive families and to batch or multi-objective settings. This perspective opens several directions for future work, including tighter boundary handling for full rotated Fisher regions and sharper theory for non-lengthscale kernels. The present work studies a theoretical aspect of BO and is intended for black-box optimization methodology; we do not foresee any broader societal impacts. 9References

## C Implementation Details 

For the experiments with a GP using an SE kernel, we use the Dimensionally Scaled LogNormal Priors (DSP) with mean √2 + log( Dx) ∗ 0.5 and standard deviation √3. DSP [6] uses dimensionally scaled LogNormal priors on the lengthscales and optimizes AF over the full input domain X , which is also the default implementation for SingleTaskGP in BoTorch v0.16.1 [15]. For the experiments with the GP using an IBNN kernel, we keep the BoTorch default depth 3.TuRBO-REI combines TuRBO’s lengthscale-weighted trust region [11] with Regional Expected Improvement for choosing the trust-region center when the TR collapses [41]. Namura and Takemori [41] showed that REI improved TuRBO by selecting a new center in a region with high expected utility. BOUNCE [36] uses an adaptive subspace decomposition and can optimize mixed and combinatorial structures as well. FITR keeps the same trust-region scaling logic as TuRBO-REI (Algorithm 3) and also uses REI center selection at trust-region collapse. It replaces ARD lengthscale weights by the regularized diagonal pullback-Fisher weights from Algorithm 1. Section 5 makes clear that the main method in this paper is the diagonal axis-aligned approximation; we also test a full-tensor ablation (FullFITR). FullFITR uses whitening for Sobol sampling and a simple boundary-clipping strategy instead of polytope MCMC. Due to its additional computational cost, we evaluate FullFITR only on a subset of problems. DSP, TuRBO-REI, FITR, and FullFITR are implemented in BoTorch [15] and use LogEI [1]. Inputs are normalized to [0 , 1] Dx , and outputs are standardized before fitting the GP. For each experiment, we start from 30 randomly sampled observed points. We use sequential evaluations ( q = 1 ). The SE-kernel experiments are repeated over 11 independently seeded trials, whereas the IBNN experiments 15 are repeated over 5 independently seeded trials. 256 raw samples. For FITR, the diagonal entries of the pullback Fisher tensor gt(xt,c ) are estimated with S = 256 MC samples using (9). These settings are fixed across tasks rather than tuned per benchmark. The damping parameter is chosen automatically as εt = D−1
